**Source:** Daily Trust

Headline inflation, which peaked above 34% in 2024, has fallen for several consecutive months, reaching 15.43% in July 2026, down from 15.91% in June. The naira has firmed to N1,346 to N1,349 per dollar in the official market in August 2026, its strongest in five months, with a narrowed parallel market premium. External reserves hit a seventeen-year high of $52.66 billion by August 19, 2026, increasing over $7 billion since the year's start. Real GDP grew by 3.89% year-on-year in Q1 2026, driven by the non-oil economy. However, food inflation remains high at 20.31% in July. The CBN's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) has maintained the benchmark rate at 26.5% since February 2026 (a 50 basis point cut from 27%), alongside a Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) of 45% for banks. The article argues that while price stability is a developmental achievement, orthodoxy alone cannot address supply-driven inflation caused by factors like transport costs, insecurity, and agricultural productivity.

**Deep Lore / Context:** This comprehensive analysis offers a crucial update to the **"CBN Policy and Economic Management"** storyline, which we have been tracking as "escalating" due to the significant reforms and their impact on economic stability. The past three years have indeed seen Nigeria undergo one of its most challenging periods of macroeconomic adjustment, with the Tinubu administration inheriting a complex economic landscape. The removal of the petrol subsidy and the unification of the foreign exchange market were bold, albeit painful, decisions aimed at restoring fiscal sanity and attracting foreign investment. The positive indicators cited – falling headline inflation, naira appreciation, and surging external reserves – are direct consequences of these policies and the CBN's restrictive monetary stance. This suggests that the initial sacrifices are beginning to yield the intended stabilization effects, providing a foundation for potential growth.

This distinction is vital because, as the author argues, relying solely on interest rate hikes to combat supply-side issues can stifle investment and consumption without addressing the root causes.
