### Why Nigeria Cannot Tax Its Way to a Trillion Dollar Economy
**Source:** Nairametrics

**What happened**
A Nairametrics opinion piece today argues that Nigeria's ambition to achieve a trillion-dollar economy cannot be realized solely by increasing its tax-to-GDP ratio. While the government aims to raise this ratio from 10% in 2023 to 18% within a few years, the author, Frank Nnamka, contends that focusing on extraction rather than development is a fundamental error. He highlights that rich countries became wealthy first, then increased their tax take, not the other way around. The article acknowledges recent reforms that have doubled tax collected in naira terms in two years and broadened the tax base, but warns that if the pressure to hit the 18% target leads to leaning harder on formal businesses and salaried workers while the informal majority remains untaxed, it will stifle productivity and investment, ultimately derailing the trillion-dollar goal. The real solution, it suggests, is to formalize the informal economy by registering businesses, titling land, and recording transactions, thereby growing a base worth taxing.

**Deep Lore / Context**
This analysis provides crucial context to our "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" storyline, which has been escalating with economic challenges like rising costs and brain drain, and also connects directly to "CBN Policy and Economic Management." The government's fiscal target is a well-intentioned response to Nigeria's low revenue generation, a perennial issue that limits public investment in critical infrastructure, education, and healthcare. However, the article's critique is profound: it challenges the very philosophy behind the current tax reform efforts. Historically, nations like South Korea and China achieved rapid growth with lower tax takes relative to their economies, demonstrating that a broad, formal, and productive economy must precede a high tax-to-GDP ratio. Nigeria's vast informal sector, estimated to be over 60% of the economy, remains largely untaxed, creating an uneven playing field and placing undue burden on the formal sector.

The distinction between building a "bigger tax state" and a "bigger taxable economy" is fundamental. The former risks stifling the productive elements of the economy, potentially leading to capital flight and discouraging investment, which would exacerbate the existing "funding challenges" within the "Nigerian tech ecosystem (startups, fintech)." The latter, by formalizing economic activities, not only expands the tax base but also fosters accountability between the state and its citizens, as taxpayers demand to see what their money buys. This is a long-term development project, requiring robust legal frameworks, digital infrastructure for record-keeping, and a concerted effort to bring informal businesses into the formal fold. For investors and businesses, the signal to watch is not just the rising tax take, but the *mix* – whether the base is truly widening or if the compliant few are simply being squeezed harder. This will determine if Nigeria is becoming more, or less, investable.

### “Twenty Years of Brand Building Simply Froze in Time”: How Coding Agents Select Their Tools of Choice
**Source:** The New Stack

**What happened**
A study by developer tool growth services company Armature reveals a profound shift in how software tools are selected, driven by the rise of AI coding agents like Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor. The research, based on 17,000 tool choice sessions, found that "twenty years of brand building carried out by tool vendors simply froze in time." Agents often default to established brands like Docker but may fail to pick the tool if its current offerings aren't clearly articulated for an AI. Armature co-founder Theodore Otzenberger states that the agent is now the decision-maker, with "life-or-death impact" on tool vendors who must ensure their products are "mentioned, picked and elevated to must-have status" by agents. The study also found that repository context is key, with different programming languages leading to different tool choices (e.g., Resend for TypeScript, SendGrid for Python). Furthermore, different agents use different sources for their decisions, with Cursor relying heavily on the web, Codex almost always using web search with specific operators, and Claude Code primarily using its priors. Glokal AI OÜ Founder & CTO Jeet Pattanaik called this "procurement arriving through the back door," noting that "getting mentioned isn’t the same as winning," and what tips an agent's decision is often "unnervingly small," emphasizing the need to optimize documentation and pricing pages for non-human readers.

**Deep Lore / Context**
This article represents a significant escalation in our "Generative Software and Interface World Models" and "AI Agents in Enterprise Observability" storylines, fundamentally altering how we think about software development and the developer tools ecosystem. The shift from human-centric Search Engine Optimization (SEO) to Answer Engine Optimization (AEO) and Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) for influencing LLMs is a game-changer. For decades, brand recognition, community buzz, and human-readable marketing were paramount for developer tool adoption. Now, with AI agents increasingly taking over end-to-end tasks, the "decision makers are changing," and the criteria for tool selection are becoming algorithmic. This means that a tool's documentation, API clarity, and how well its capabilities are articulated in a machine-readable format will become far more critical than traditional marketing. The observation that "PayPal was cited 139 times and never picked" while "LangChain was the most-mentioned framework at 194 times, but it was only chosen four times" is a stark warning to vendors.

The implications for the future of software engineering are profound. As a web developer transitioning into AI, understanding this shift is paramount. The "repository context is key" finding, where TypeScript codebases led to Resend being picked, while Python led to SendGrid, suggests that agents are becoming highly context-aware and language-specific in their tool recommendations. This means that developers will need to understand not just how to *use* tools, but how to *present* them to AI agents, and how to structure their projects in ways that facilitate optimal agent-driven tool selection. The "procurement arriving through the back door" analogy is apt; AI agents are effectively becoming automated procurement officers, making choices based on optimized documentation and pricing pages designed for non-human readers. This new paradigm will necessitate a re-evaluation of how software is built, documented, and integrated, pushing developers to think about "agent-first" design principles for their toolchains.
