Makinde Expresses Regret Over 2023 Tinubu Alliance, Cites Failure to Deliver National Unity and Competent Government

Source: Vanguard

What happened: Oyo State Governor, Seyi Makinde, publicly admitted his regret over his political alliance with President Bola Tinubu ahead of the 2023 general election. Speaking during a live radio interview in Ibadan, Makinde stated that the alliance failed to deliver the national unity, fairness, and competent government he had expected. He clarified that his support for Tinubu, then the APC presidential candidate, was based on the belief that the administration would prioritize national interest, bring together capable Nigerians from diverse backgrounds, and address the country’s challenges without narrow political or regional lines. He specifically mentioned that the current administration is not a "government of national competence" or "national unity."

Deep Lore / Context: This is a significant development, closing a loop on the complex political realignments that shaped the 2023 presidential election. Governor Makinde was a prominent member of the 'G5' or 'Integrity Group' within the Peoples Democratic Party (PDP), which rebelled against their party's presidential candidate, Atiku Abubakar, over issues of regional balance and fairness. Their core demand was that the PDP National Chairman, Iyorchia Ayu (a northerner), should resign to allow a southerner to take the position, following the emergence of Atiku (also a northerner) as the presidential flagbearer. When this demand was not met, Makinde and others withheld support for Atiku, with Makinde openly endorsing Tinubu.

Makinde's expression of regret now signals a potential fracturing of the alliances that brought the current administration to power and underscores the deep-seated political calculations and expectations that underpin Nigerian politics. His remarks about the failure to form a "government of national unity" or "competence" resonate with broader public sentiment in some quarters regarding the administration's performance. This move could be seen as Makinde positioning himself for future political maneuvers, especially given his own declaration of presidential ambition in May 2026 under an alliance between the PDP and the Allied Peoples Movement (APM). It highlights the fluid nature of political loyalties and the constant recalibration of strategies as the 2027 elections draw closer, intensifying the "Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)" storyline.

Peter Obi Faults Tinubu’s 30-Day Petrol Discount, Calls Policy Reactive and Poorly Sequenced

Source: Vanguard

What happened: Peter Obi, the presidential candidate of the Nigeria Democratic Congress (NDC), has sharply criticized the federal government's recently announced 30-day petrol discount. Obi, in a post on X, labeled the initiative as a "reactive policy" that fails to genuinely address the severe economic hardship faced by Nigerians. He argued that the temporary discount, which he noted is less than five percent of fuel costs, is an attempt to substitute "political optics for sound policy." Obi recalled that President Tinubu removed the petrol subsidy on May 29, 2023, without adequate consultation or assessment of its potential impact, and also faulted the subsequent floating of the naira and tax reforms as poorly sequenced and implemented. He questioned whether the discount would reduce poverty, unemployment, insecurity, and inequality, or increase productivity and exports, and speculated if it was a test for subsidy restoration or politically motivated for 2027.

Deep Lore / Context: This critique from Peter Obi directly addresses the ongoing economic reforms initiated by the Tinubu administration, particularly the removal of the petrol subsidy and the unification of the foreign exchange market. These policies, while praised by international bodies like the World Bank for improving macroeconomic stability and increasing government revenues (as we saw in today's World Bank report), have simultaneously led to significant economic hardship for ordinary Nigerians, characterized by soaring inflation and increased cost of living. This divergence between macroeconomic indicators and public sentiment has been a persistent theme in the "CBN Policy and Economic Management" and "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" storylines.

Obi's strong stance reflects the opposition's consistent narrative that the government's reforms, while perhaps well-intentioned, lack a human-centric approach and have exacerbated poverty. His call for detailed regulatory impact assessments highlights a demand for greater transparency and public engagement in policy formulation. The "30-day petrol discount" itself is a recent government measure, likely intended to assuage public anger over fuel prices, but Obi's immediate dismissal of it as "reactive" and insufficient underscores the deep skepticism from the opposition and a significant portion of the populace. This ongoing debate about the efficacy and impact of economic policies will undoubtedly continue to shape political discourse and public trust as the country grapples with its economic challenges and heads towards the 2027 elections.

Tinubu Welcomes World Bank Report, Says Economic Reforms are Delivering Results

Source: Daily Trust, Channels TV

What happened: President Bola Ahmed Tinubu has welcomed the World Bank’s October 2026 Nigeria Development Update, asserting that its findings confirm his administration’s economic reforms are on track and placing the economy on a strong footing. The report, titled "Beyond the Federal Purse: How Higher Revenues Reshaped State Priorities," found that Nigeria's poverty rate has stabilized for the first time since 2019 and is expected to decline as GDP outpaces population growth. According to a statement from Bayo Onanuga, the President’s Special Adviser on Information and Strategy, the economy grew 4.2 percent in the first half of 2026, up from 3.9 percent in H1 2025, with projections of at least 4.4 percent growth between 2026 and 2028. Inflation, which fell from 27.6 percent in January 2025 to 15.2 percent in December 2025, is expected to ease to about 12 percent by 2028 despite global fuel price impacts. The report credited reforms since 2023 with raising federation revenues by 69 percent in real terms between 2023 and 2025, with states being the largest beneficiaries. Public debt is also projected to fall from 40.0 percent of GDP in 2025 to 38.1 percent in 2026.

Deep Lore / Context: This announcement is a significant moment for the Tinubu administration, as it provides external validation for the difficult economic reforms initiated since May 2023, particularly the removal of the petrol subsidy and the unification of the foreign exchange market. These reforms, while leading to increased government revenues and a more stable macroeconomic outlook (as reflected in Fitch's positive outlook revision, a storyline we've been tracking), have also inflicted considerable hardship on ordinary Nigerians through high inflation and increased cost of living. The World Bank's report, therefore, serves as a crucial piece of evidence for the government to counter criticisms about the immediate negative impacts of its policies.

The report's findings on poverty stabilization and projected decline, along with revenue increases for states and a falling public debt-to-GDP ratio, are key metrics the government will leverage to demonstrate progress. This directly feeds into the "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" and "CBN Policy and Economic Management" storylines, indicating that from a macroeconomic perspective, the reforms are yielding desired results. However, the President's acknowledgment that "more work remains to ensure they fully translate into better living standards for every household, starting with lower food prices and decent jobs for our young people" highlights the ongoing challenge of translating macroeconomic stability into tangible improvements for citizens, a point consistently raised by opposition figures like Peter Obi. Nigerian Startups Face Funding Gap Between Seed and Growth Stages, Sage Grey MD Warns

Source: Nairametrics

What happened: Temitope Runsewe, Managing Director and CEO of Sage Grey Finance Limited, has warned that Nigerian startups are encountering a significant funding gap between early-stage investments and the larger capital needed for scaling. He told Nairametrics that investors are becoming more selective, demanding stronger evidence of product-market fit, revenue growth, sensible unit economics, and a credible path to profitability. This shift, he explained, could create a "missing middle" where promising businesses struggle to secure the patient capital required to bridge the gap between small seed investments and large institutional funding rounds. Runsewe believes this could encourage founders to focus on building viable, revenue-generating businesses earlier, rather than solely relying on fundraising.

Deep Lore / Context: This warning from Sage Grey's MD is a critical update to the "Nigerian tech ecosystem (startups, fintech)" storyline, highlighting a maturing but also challenging funding environment. For years, the Nigerian and broader African tech scene benefited from a surge of global liquidity, allowing many startups to raise significant capital based on growth potential rather than immediate profitability. However, as global economic conditions tighten and investor sentiment shifts towards prudence, the emphasis is now firmly on sustainable business models. This is not necessarily a negative development, as Runsewe notes, as it pushes entrepreneurs to build "businesses rather than simply build fundraising propositions."

The "missing middle" phenomenon is a common challenge in developing startup ecosystems, where angel and seed funding might be available, and later-stage institutional capital can be attracted by proven scale, but the bridge funding for rapid expansion is scarce. This situation is further complicated by the fact that while overall funding for African tech startups has seen debt financing increase (41% in 2025, up from 17% in 2019), equity funding has declined. This means founders need to be more strategic about their capital structure. The implication for the ecosystem is a potential slowdown in the scaling of innovative solutions if this gap is not addressed by local capital providers such as venture capital funds, private equity firms, family offices, and development finance institutions, which Runsewe identifies as crucial players in filling this void.
