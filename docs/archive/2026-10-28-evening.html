NITDA Tightens Cybersecurity Rules for MDAs, Mandates DNS Security Assessments

Source: Nairametrics

What happened The National Information Technology Development Agency (NITDA) has implemented stricter cybersecurity regulations for all Ministries, Departments and Agencies (MDAs), making Domain Name System Security Extensions (DNSSEC) a mandatory component of its cybersecurity assessment criteria. This initiative aims to fortify government digital infrastructure against increasing cyber threats. NITDA's Director General, Kashifu Inuwa Abdullahi, represented by Oluwasegun Falayan, announced this policy at a cybersecurity awareness webinar organized by the Nigeria Internet Registration Association (NiRA). Abdullahi emphasized that government institutions must not only register their .gov.ng domain names but also ensure they are "secured and must be signed." NiRA President, Adesola Akinsanya, highlighted the economic implications of weak digital security, citing Check Point figures that show Nigerian organizations faced an average of 4,361 attempted cyberattacks weekly in June, significantly higher than the global average of 2,270. He also referenced Nigeria Inter Bank Settlement System (NIBSS) figures, revealing that digital payment fraud amounted to N25.85 billion in 2025.

Deep Lore / Context This move by NITDA directly addresses the escalating concerns within the Adversarial AI Evolution and Cybersecurity storyline. The persistent rise in cyber threats, as evidenced by the staggering 4,361 weekly attempted attacks on Nigerian organizations, necessitates proactive and robust defense mechanisms. NITDA's mandate for DNSSEC, which uses digital signatures to verify domain name authenticity and protect against certain cyberattacks, is a critical step in strengthening the country's digital foundation. This follows previous discussions about the increasing sophistication of AI in security breaches and the need for advanced cybersecurity measures. The inclusion of DNS security in NITDA's assessment framework signifies a concrete policy action to enhance national cyber resilience, moving beyond mere awareness to mandatory implementation. The NIBSS figures on digital payment fraud further underscore the economic imperative behind these security enhancements, linking directly to the broader National Progress & Economy (Nigeria) storyline and the need to protect the digital economy.

The policy also implicitly touches upon the AI Governance and Regulation Gap, as secure digital infrastructure is a prerequisite for responsible AI deployment and data handling. While DNSSEC itself isn't AI, it's a foundational security layer that protects the data and systems AI models might interact with or be built upon. The caution from NiRA President Adesola Akinsanya about implementing DNSSEC without necessary technical expertise creating new problems highlights the ongoing challenge of skill gaps and the need for careful, coordinated implementation. This echoes concerns about widespread AI adoption without adequate infrastructure and human capacity. NITDA and NiRA are key actors here, with NITDA as the regulator and NiRA as the domain registry and capacity builder. Their collaboration aims to build a more resilient digital ecosystem, which is crucial for fostering trust in online services and protecting citizens and businesses from financial and data loss. This policy is a direct response to the evolving threat landscape and a necessary step towards securing Nigeria's digital future.

Source: BusinessDay

What happened Flutterwave, the African financial technology startup valued at $3 billion, is pivoting its growth strategy from digital payments to banking. After a decade primarily operating as a payment gateway, the Nigerian fintech plans to offer direct deposits, savings, and commercial lending. This strategic shift follows the company's acquisition of a Nigerian banking license in April, which now permits it to hold customer deposits directly. CEO Olugbenga "GB" Agboola stated at the Moonshot technology conference, "Our claim to fame is obviously payments... But if you can trust us with your payments, it is a much easier sell for us to keep the money for you. You can earn, pay, and then save." Flutterwave, which has processed over $40 billion across more than one billion transactions, views pure payment processing as having reached a "natural plateau." The company plans a phased rollout of credit, beginning with working capital loans for its existing merchant base, and aims to expand its banking services to Ghana and Kenya.

Deep Lore / Context This strategic pivot by Flutterwave is a major development in the CBN Policy and Economic Management and National Progress & Economy (Nigeria) storylines. It signifies a maturation of the fintech sector and a response to evolving market dynamics. The CBN's proactive approval of JP Morgan's merchant bank, as noted in a previous digest, signaled a commitment to financial sector stability and foreign investment. Flutterwave's move to acquire a banking license and diversify into core banking services aligns with this broader trend of strengthening the financial ecosystem. It reflects a growing recognition among mature fintechs that transaction fees alone may not sustain long-term, high-margin growth, pushing them towards more comprehensive financial offerings. This shift could intensify competition within the traditional banking sector while simultaneously expanding access to financial services for underserved populations, particularly SMEs.

The decision to expand into Ghana and Kenya from day one reinforces Flutterwave's vision as a "pan-African company," connecting to the broader narrative of African tech leadership. Entering the credit market, while offering higher profit margins, also introduces increased exposure to default risks and non-performing loans (NPLs). Flutterwave's plan to mitigate this by starting with working capital loans for its existing merchant base demonstrates a calculated approach to risk management. This evolution of fintechs into full-fledged financial institutions is a critical trend for Nigeria's economy, potentially driving financial inclusion, stimulating economic activity through accessible credit, and further digitizing the financial landscape.
