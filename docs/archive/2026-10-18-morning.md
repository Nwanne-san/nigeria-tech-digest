### SERAP Sues INEC Over Failure to Account for ‘Missing ₦126bn Electoral Funds’
**Source:** Punch, Channels TV, Premium Times

**What happened:**
The Socio-Economic Rights and Accountability Project (SERAP) has filed a lawsuit against the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC) at the Federal High Court in Abuja. SERAP is seeking an order of mandamus to compel INEC to disclose detailed information regarding the expenditure, utilization, and disbursement of over ₦126.46 billion. These funds were appropriated for electoral operations, including the procurement of ballot boxes, electoral devices, materials, result sheets, vehicles, and accreditation devices, as well as audit and consultancy services, which SERAP alleges were diverted, lost, mismanaged, or otherwise unaccounted for. The allegations stem from the 2023 audited report of the Auditor-General of the Federation, published on August 7, 2026, covering periods between January and December 2022, and in some cases, extending to December 31, 2023.

**Deep Lore / Context:**
This lawsuit marks a significant escalation in the ongoing scrutiny of Nigeria's electoral processes and the integrity of its institutions. It directly follows the ongoing storyline of **"Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)"**, which has seen President Tinubu nominate INEC officials ahead of the 2027 elections and early endorsements signaling the start of election campaigns. The Auditor-General's report detailed several alarming irregularities, including over ₦112 billion "irregularly paid" for electoral materials without competitive bidding or a BPP Certificate of No Objection, with "no evidence that the items were procured" and contracts awarded to companies with unknown competences and addresses. This suggests a systemic failure in procurement processes and raises serious questions about the transparency and accountability of an institution critical to Nigeria's democracy. The timing, with the 2027 general elections approaching, makes these allegations particularly potent, as public confidence in INEC's ability to conduct credible elections is paramount.

The implications of these allegations are far-reaching. If proven, such mismanagement of funds could severely undermine the credibility of future elections and erode public trust in governance. SERAP's action highlights the crucial role of civil society organizations in demanding accountability from public institutions, especially in a democracy still grappling with issues of corruption and transparency. The suit also points to alleged payments of over ₦1 billion for Toyota Prado TXL 2021 models without proper bidding, over ₦3 billion paid to contractors *before* contracts were awarded for sensitive materials, and over ₦9 billion for 22 contracts split to circumvent procurement procedures. These detailed allegations, if not adequately addressed by INEC, could fuel cynicism about the electoral system and potentially lead to increased political instability as the nation gears up for another election cycle. The case will be closely watched as a test of the judiciary's independence and the government's commitment to fighting corruption.

### Patrick Dele Cole: The Stories Behind the Daily Times, The Guardian and the Corridors of Power
**Source:** BusinessDay

**What happened:**
Patrick Dele Cole, a prominent Nigerian academic, civil servant, diplomat, and media executive, passed away on Saturday, October 17, 2026, at the age of 86. His life story, as recounted in a revealing interview with Richard Ikiebe, offers a rare glimpse into the workings of Nigeria’s political establishment and the foundational struggles of its influential newspapers. Cole was initially appointed to the Daily Times board in 1975 by the military government to promote its transition program but instead became a staunch protector of journalists, ensuring their independence from government influence. He later helped lay the groundwork for The Guardian newspaper.

**Deep Lore / Context:**
Cole's career trajectory is a powerful narrative about the tensions between power and truth, especially during Nigeria's formative years under military rule. His appointment to the Daily Times was a calculated move by the government to control the narrative around its "Nine Steps to Civil Rule" program. However, Cole, a former academic from Cambridge, chose a different path, prioritizing journalistic integrity over government directives. This stance was particularly courageous given the history of the Daily Times, which had previously faced closure and the arrest of its top journalists under the Gowon administration for challenging authority. Cole's commitment to protecting journalists from military intimidation and his refusal to dictate editorial content helped cement the newspaper's reputation as an independent institution, even under government ownership.

His experiences highlight the critical role of a free press in a developing democracy and the personal sacrifices involved in upholding such principles. Cole's insistence that "the only way to gain the confidence of anybody working with you is to be able to tell and show that you can protect him or her" speaks volumes about the leadership required to foster an environment of independent thought and reporting. His life story, therefore, serves as a historical blueprint for understanding the complex interplay between governance, media freedom, and individual conviction in Nigeria. It underscores the ongoing importance of robust media institutions and ethical leadership in shaping national discourse and holding power accountable, a lesson that remains profoundly relevant today amidst debates on misinformation and media influence.
