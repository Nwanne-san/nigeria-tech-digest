Benchers Back CJN’s Push to Retire Colonial Legal Titles and Practices

Source: Vanguard What happened: The Chief Justice of Nigeria (CJN), Justice Kudirat Kekere-Ekun, has issued a memo dated July 13, 2026, barring the use of the title "Barrister" as a prefix to lawyers' names in all official correspondence, deeming it inappropriate and inconsistent with professional standards. This directive follows concerns raised by the Body of Benchers (BoB), whose Chairman, HRM Albert Akpomuje, SAN, described the use of "Barrister" as a title as "very wrong and unprofessional," particularly among political figures who are not in active practice. The BoB emphasized that competence, comportment, hard work, and diligence should define a lawyer, not an appellation. This move is part of a broader push to reform Nigeria's legal practice by retiring colonial-era relics, drawing parallels with Kenya, Malawi, and Burkina Faso, which have already moved away from traditional wigs, robes, and honorifics like "My Lord." Deep Lore / Context: This development reflects a growing nationalistic sentiment within Nigeria's legal system, aiming to shed the vestiges of colonial rule and foster a more indigenous and practical approach to legal practice. The "Barrister" title, along with wigs and robes, originated from 17th-century British fashion and legal traditions, where a "Bar" separated legal practitioners from the public. In the English model, legal practice is split between barristers (advocacy specialists) and solicitors (client relations and preparation). However, Nigeria, having adopted English legal concepts since the Lagos Supreme Court Ordinance of 1863, fused these roles, making anyone called to its Bar both a "Barrister and Solicitor." Over time, "Barrister" evolved into a common professional title and cultural status marker, despite the Legal Practitioners Disciplinary Committee (LPDC) describing its use as "unprofessional and improper" in a 2017 ruling (NBA v. Ofomata).

The CJN's directive, supported by the Body of Benchers, is a significant step towards modernizing the judiciary and aligning it with contemporary professional standards, moving beyond mere symbolism to address the substance of legal practice. While some dissenting voices have questioned the CJN's administrative powers to restrict a designation recognized under the Legal Practitioners Act, citing a Court of Appeal decision (Ishaku & Anor v. Kantiok & Ors 2011) that affirmed the prefix does not alter legal identity, the momentum for reform appears strong. This initiative connects to broader discussions about national identity, decolonization of institutions, and the pursuit of efficiency and transparency in governance. The hope is that this will pave the way for more comprehensive judicial reform, including the purging of outdated colonial-era language in legal drafting, ultimately contributing to a more effective and purpose-driven legal system in Nigeria.
