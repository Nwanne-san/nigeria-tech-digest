CBN Research Confirms Budget Deficits Drive Naira Depreciation and Interest Rate Pressure

Source: Nairametrics

What happened A significant study published in the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) Journal of Applied Statistics, titled "Budget Deficit Dynamics and Macroeconomic Performance in Nigeria" by Ndubuisi E. Uguru, has provided compelling evidence linking budget deficits to key economic challenges. Using annual data from 1981 to 2024, the paper found that budget deficits are a primary driver of exchange-rate depreciation, accounting for a large share of short-run currency moves and consistently pushing the naira weaker over time. The study also established that deficits put delayed upward pressure on interest rates, as government borrowing competes with private borrowers for domestic credit. While no statistically significant direct link was found between deficits and inflation, the research suggests inflation is driven by supply shocks, structural bottlenecks, and the pass-through effect of a weaker naira into local prices. This pattern is termed "fiscal dominance," where the government's financing needs constrain the central bank's ability to achieve monetary stability. The naira closed at N1,350.45 per dollar on November 5, weaker than N1,346.01 on November 2, despite net foreign exchange reserves reaching a record $46 billion. The study recommends a legally binding fiscal rule capping the budget deficit as a share of GDP and public coordination of fiscal and monetary operations.

Deep Lore / Context This research from the CBN's own journal is a critical validation of a long-standing concern among economists and policy analysts, directly escalating the "CBN Policy and Economic Management" storyline. For months, we've tracked the conflicting narratives surrounding Nigeria's economic recovery, with government relief efforts and fintech innovations contrasted against rising business costs and a growing external debt burden. This study provides an internal, authoritative voice confirming that fiscal imbalances are at the root of naira depreciation and interest rate pressure, rather than solely external factors or the CBN's monetary policy.

The concept of "fiscal dominance" is central here. It means that even if the CBN attempts to tighten monetary policy (like the 350-basis-point MPR cut to 23% in September, which still leaves room to ease), the government's persistent need to borrow heavily undermines these efforts. This competition for funds drives up interest rates for private businesses and fuels demand for dollars as importers and portfolio managers anticipate further depreciation. The recommendation for a legally binding fiscal rule and public coordination between fiscal and monetary authorities is not new, but its endorsement by internal CBN research adds significant weight. This report underscores why the naira continues to struggle despite rising reserves, and it sets a clear agenda for structural reforms needed to achieve genuine economic stability and address the "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" challenges.
