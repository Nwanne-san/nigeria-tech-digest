### 3. Media's Role in Election Results: Transparency vs. Official Declaration

**Headline:** Can the media announce election results before INEC’s official declaration?, By Samson Itodo (Premium Times)

**What happened:** Samson Itodo, Executive Director of Yiaga Africa and Chairperson of the African Union Advisory Group on AI in Peace, Security and Governance, provided a crucial clarification on the media's role in reporting election results. He argued that while the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC) is the sole constitutional authority to *declare* election results and return a winner, media organizations can responsibly share and aggregate *officially announced results* from polling units. This distinction is vital for transparency, especially given historical concerns about manipulation during the collation stage. Itodo emphasized that public awareness of results, facilitated by media and citizen oversight, helps protect INEC from political pressure and strengthens the integrity of the electoral process. However, he cautioned against media organizations presenting their reporting as a final declaration of the election outcome, as INEC still needs to assess constitutional thresholds and account for factors like over-voting or disruptions.

**Deep Lore / Context:** This article directly addresses a critical aspect of the "Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)" storyline, which we've been tracking as escalating with the onset of the 2027 election cycle. The debate over who can announce results and when is not new; it gained significant traction during the 2023 general elections, particularly with the introduction of the INEC Result Viewing Portal (IReV). The IReV was designed to enhance transparency by making polling unit results publicly accessible, but its implementation and the subsequent challenges in real-time uploading led to controversies and accusations of result manipulation. Itodo's piece serves as a timely reminder of the legal and ethical boundaries for media in an environment where public trust in electoral processes is often fragile.

The core issue here is balancing transparency with the constitutional mandate of INEC. Historically, the collation centers have been points of vulnerability, where results from polling units could be altered before final declaration. Media and civil society organizations, by reporting results as they are announced at the grassroots, aim to create a parallel, verifiable record that can deter manipulation and hold INEC accountable. This proactive approach by the media and civil society is seen as a safeguard against electoral malpractice. However, the caution against "declaring a winner" prematurely is equally important, as it acknowledges INEC's complex role in applying constitutional thresholds (e.g., 25% of votes in two-thirds of states/LGAs) and addressing anomalies. This ongoing tension between public transparency and official authority is a defining characteristic of Nigeria's electoral system, and responsible media engagement is crucial for maintaining democratic accountability.
