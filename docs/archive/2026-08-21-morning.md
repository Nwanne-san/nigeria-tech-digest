### Presidency Challenges Atiku To Explain Cost, Funding Of Proposed Fuel Subsidy
**Source:** Arise TV

**What happened:** The Presidency has challenged former Vice President Atiku Abubakar to provide comprehensive fiscal and legal details regarding his proposed petroleum subsidy policy. Bayo Onanuga, Special Adviser to the President on Information and Strategy, warned that Nigeria cannot afford a return to an opaque system that could undermine public finances. Onanuga questioned the cost, funding source, and impact on federal, state, and local government revenues, recalling the previous subsidy regime's enormous pressure on the Nigerian National Petroleum Company Limited (NNPCL), which saw millions of barrels of crude pledged against loans and billions owed to suppliers by 2024. He argued that if petrol were sold substantially below its economic cost, estimated between N1,200 and N1,300 per litre, the difference would burden public finances, potentially reducing spending on infrastructure and social services, lowering allocations to states, or increasing public debt. While acknowledging the severe pressure of petrol prices on households and businesses, the Presidency maintained that sustainable relief should not involve recreating a fiscally unsustainable arrangement, instead promoting alternatives like Compressed Natural Gas (CNG).

**Deep Lore / Context:** This is a direct and forceful continuation of the **CBN Policy and Economic Management** and **National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)** storylines, specifically the ongoing economic reforms initiated by President Bola Tinubu's administration. The removal of the petrol subsidy in May 2023 was a cornerstone of Tinubu's economic agenda, aimed at freeing up national resources and stabilizing the naira, which has seen some strengthening due to improved liquidity as noted in previous digests. Atiku Abubakar's proposal to restore the subsidy, ahead of the 2027 presidential election, represents a fundamental challenge to this policy direction. The Presidency's demand for "fiscal arithmetic" underscores the deep-seated issues of transparency and sustainability that plagued the previous subsidy regime, which was often criticized for corruption and its drain on national coffers. This debate highlights the persistent tension between the immediate economic relief sought by citizens facing high living costs and the long-term fiscal stability pursued by the government.

The actors involved are clear: the Presidency, defending its current economic strategy, and Atiku Abubakar, positioning himself as an alternative with a different approach to alleviating public hardship. The incentives are equally clear: the government aims to demonstrate fiscal responsibility and the benefits of its reforms, while the opposition seeks to capitalize on public discontent over the cost of living. The escalation history of this issue dates back to the very first day of the Tinubu administration, making it a central and recurring theme in Nigeria's political and economic discourse. The outcome of this debate, and the eventual public perception of the subsidy removal's long-term benefits versus short-term pains, will significantly shape Nigeria's economic trajectory and political landscape in the coming years.

### Debian just proposed banning AI code. Here’s why it matters for open source developers & maintainers.
**Source:** The New Stack

**What happened:** The board behind the open-source operating system Debian has tabled a general resolution statement with proposals both for and against the use of Large Language Model (LLM)-assisted contributions within the project. One prominent proposal, attributed to Matthias Geiger, advocates for an outright ban, asserting that widespread LLM usage stems from a "move fast and break things" attitude, which is "contrary to what makes Debian Debian, and is inappropriate for Debian contributors." This ban, if approved, would apply to Debian source packages, official web resources, and direct code contributions like packaging, native Debian software, documentation, and translations. However, it would not apply to upstream software projects that use generative AI, nor to patches and security fixes from such sources. Seven counterarguments were also tabled, including allowing conditional AI contributions, updating the code of conduct, accepting AI contributions for Debian-specific work, and even a proposal citing "climate destruction" as a reason to avoid LLMs. Voting on these proposals closes on Friday, August 28, 2026.

**Deep Lore / Context:** This debate within the Debian community is a watershed moment for the open-source world and directly impacts the **AI Governance and Regulation Gap** and **AI's Impact on Information Integrity** storylines. Debian, known for its rigorous standards, stability, and foundational role in countless systems (including the International Space Station), is grappling with fundamental questions about the nature of authorship, code quality, and the long-term health of its developer community in the age of AI. The "move fast and break things" ethos, while common in some tech sectors, clashes directly with Debian's core values, highlighting a significant cultural and philosophical divide emerging with the advent of generative AI.

Critics of a blanket ban, like Joe Phillips, argue that the debate is less about principle and more about "how big the sticker should be on code origins," emphasizing the need for declared provenance. Justin Beals, CEO of Strike Graph, views the proposed ban as an "admission that nobody has built a reliable way to verify what an AI agent actually produced before it lands in a codebase this many systems depend on." This exposes a critical vulnerability in open-source projects built on trust and reputation, where contributors can generate "infinite plausible-looking code."
