Nigeria’s stalled constitutional reform keeps power and money trapped in Abuja

Source: BusinessDay

What happened The 10th National Assembly has failed to meet its December 2025 deadline for completing its review of the 1999 Constitution. This delay leaves critical proposals concerning fiscal federalism, devolution of powers, local government autonomy, and electoral reform unresolved. While lawmakers fast-tracked the State Police amendment due to worsening insecurity, other fundamental changes that would redistribute money, authority, and responsibility between Abuja and subnational governments remain stalled. BusinessDay reported that states received N33.27 trillion between January and November 2025, 30% more than the previous year, yet 29 out of 30 states analyzed still rely on federal transfers for 70-95% of their revenue.

Deep Lore / Context This story directly continues the "Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)" and "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" storylines, both marked as escalating. The debate over Nigeria's constitutional structure, particularly fiscal federalism and devolution of powers, has been a persistent feature of its post-military democratic era. The 1999 Constitution, a product of military rule, is often criticized for its highly centralized structure, which concentrates significant power and revenue at the federal level. This centralization is seen by many as a root cause of economic inefficiency, state dependence on federal allocations, and a lack of accountability at subnational levels. Previous National Assemblies have also attempted constitutional reviews, often with similar delays and limited success in achieving fundamental restructuring. The current 10th National Assembly's promise to conclude its review by December 2025, which has now passed, underscores the deep-seated political complexities and vested interests that make such reforms challenging. The decision to fast-track State Police, while other crucial reforms like fiscal federalism and local government autonomy remain stalled, highlights a selective approach to decentralization, driven by immediate security concerns rather than a holistic vision for a more balanced federation.

The stalled reform has profound implications for Nigeria's economic development and governance. States, despite being responsible for much of the infrastructure and social services, remain heavily reliant on federal allocations, with FAAC accounting for 73.8% of revenue for 30 states in the first half of 2025 (excluding Lagos). This fiscal mismatch means states lack the independent revenue to finance their responsibilities, perpetuating a cycle of dependence and limiting their capacity for self-driven development. The Supreme Court's 2024 decision on local government financial autonomy was a step towards decentralization, but institutional weaknesses persist, with only seven of 774 LGAs having functional websites. The debate is not just about legal language but about who controls money, power, and responsibility, directly impacting businesses through regulation, taxation, and infrastructure provision. As the 2027 elections approach, the political window for comprehensive reform is narrowing, risking "constitutional reform fatigue" among citizens who have participated in consultations without seeing tangible change.

LLM Judges Verify Presence, Not Absence: Omission Blindness in AI Clinical Notes

Source: Hacker News

What happened New research titled "LLM Judges Verify Presence, Not Absence: Omission Blindness in AI Clinical Notes and What Recovers It" reveals a critical flaw in how LLM judges evaluate AI-generated clinical notes. While these judges are effective at detecting added or altered content, they are "omission blind," meaning they fail to reliably flag missing information that was established during an encounter. The study, using a benchmark of 500 single-error note pairs, found that standard LLM judge designs scored 0.50-0.63 on omissions, compared to 0.79-0.94 on added or altered content. Restructuring the task, such as a "per-fact pipeline" or a GEPA-evolved prompt that lists facts and then checks the note, significantly improved omission detection, with the single-call method detecting more (36.9% vs. 24.6%) at a tenth of the cost.

Deep Lore / Context This research paper directly addresses a critical challenge within the "AI Governance and Regulation Gap" and "AI Agents in Enterprise Observability" storylines, both escalating. As AI, particularly LLMs, becomes more integrated into sensitive applications like healthcare (e.g., ambient AI scribes for clinical notes), the reliability and safety of their outputs become paramount. The paper identifies a significant flaw: LLM judges are good at detecting added or altered content but are "omission blind," meaning they struggle to detect missing information. This is a subtle but dangerous form of error, especially in clinical settings where a missed diagnosis or omitted symptom could have severe consequences.
