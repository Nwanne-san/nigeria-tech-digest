Why CBN’s Rate Cuts Haven’t Lowered the Real Price of Credit

Source: Nairametrics

What happened: Despite the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) reducing its policy rate from 27% to 23% over the past year (September to September), the rate Nigerian banks charge their riskiest borrowers actually increased from 29.84% to 32.62%. This means the real cost of money, when adjusted for inflation which fell from 20.98% to 14.63% in the same period, effectively doubled for businesses. The article highlights that this unpredictability in the cost of money prevents businesses from making long-term commitments, leading to shorter investment horizons and a preference for projects with quick paybacks. The CBN's own data shows a wide disparity in lending rates across banks and sectors, making it difficult for firms to ascertain the actual price of money. Governor Olayemi Cardoso's recent statement that the bank "don't know" when single-digit inflation will be reached, and his characterization of a 350 basis point rate cut as a "reset" rather than a cut, further contribute to market uncertainty.

Deep Lore / Context: This report offers a critical and detailed perspective on our escalating CBN Policy and Economic Management storyline, directly challenging the effectiveness of recent monetary policy actions. The core issue is not just the high cost of credit, but its unpredictability, which fundamentally undermines long-term economic planning and investment. The CBN's efforts to bring down inflation and stabilize the naira, including floating the currency and removing fuel subsidies, have been costly but have shown some disinflationary trends and strengthened reserves. However, the disconnect between the policy rate and actual lending rates, as well as the slow transmission of policy easing to borrowers, points to deeper structural issues within Nigeria's financial system.

The "Deep Lore" here involves a long-standing challenge of monetary policy transmission in Nigeria. Banks often cite high operational costs, significant non-performing loan risks, weak contract enforcement, and insufficient credit information as reasons for maintaining high lending rates, regardless of policy rate adjustments. The article's observation that only about one in twenty-five small and medium businesses has a bank loan underscores the severe credit crunch faced by the engine of the economy, forcing many to rely on self-funding or short-term, high-interest informal credit. This situation directly impacts our National Progress & Economy (Nigeria) storyline, as an economy where long-term commitments are unfinanceable cannot achieve sustained, compound growth. Large-scale infrastructure projects like transmission lines, gas plants, or housing estates, which require decade-long bets on the cost of money, become unfeasible. The CBN's communication style, as highlighted by Governor Cardoso's remarks, while perhaps honest, inadvertently adds to market uncertainty, making it harder for businesses to forecast and plan. This creates a vicious cycle where uncertainty leads to short-termism, which in turn stifles the kind of foundational investments needed for robust economic development.

Anthropic’s IPO: A Bellwether for AI’s Circular Financing and Reputation

Sources: TechCrunch, The Verge, Semafor

What happened: Anthropic, a leading AI startup, made its public market debut today with the largest IPO of all time, raising $81.3 billion and valuing the firm at $1.5 trillion. This monumental listing, which included a private placement bringing total capital to $107 billion, is seen as a crucial bellwether for the AI boom and has helped reverse a recent tech downturn. However, the IPO also intensified scrutiny on AI's "circular financing" model, where major infrastructure partners like Amazon and Google, who are also Anthropic's biggest suppliers, hold significant stakes (Amazon's 14.7% stake valued at $159 billion, Google's 13.7% at nearly $149 billion). These companies commit billions to Anthropic, which then spends heavily on their compute and infrastructure, creating a flow of money that raises skepticism. Anthropic itself has committed to spend $900 billion, primarily on compute and infrastructure, according to regulatory filings.

Beyond the financial aspects, the IPO puts Anthropic's "good guy" reputation, built on prioritizing AI safety, on the line. The company was founded by ex-OpenAI researchers who left over safety concerns and is structured as a Public Benefit Corporation with a Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT) designed to balance mission against shareholder profit. However, critics argue this structure is shaky, with founders retaining controlling voting rights and the LTBT's power being limited and its members lacking deep AI expertise. Former Anthropic employees and corporate governance experts express skepticism about the LTBT's ability to genuinely check leadership's power, especially under increased public market pressure to generate revenue and accelerate product releases. Anthropic's CEO, Daniela Amodei, acknowledged compute as "the deepest business dilemma" facing the company, highlighting the immense resources needed for R&D and customer inference.

Deep Lore / Context: This consolidated story represents a monumental moment in the escalating storyline of AI Model Competition and Enterprise Adoption, and critically, it brings to the forefront the challenges of AI Governance and Regulation Gap. Anthropic's IPO is not just a financial milestone; it's a litmus test for the sustainability and ethical direction of the entire generative AI industry. The "circular financing" model, where cloud providers and chip manufacturers invest heavily in AI startups that then become their largest customers, has been a subject of debate. While some view it as a symbiotic relationship accelerating innovation, others see it as an artificial inflation of valuations and a concentration of power, where the same money cycles through a closed ecosystem. The IPO's success, however, suggests a shift, with public market investors and corporate bonds now expected to foot the bill, potentially making the financing system more self-sustaining. This could de-risk the compute suppliers, as their investments are now liquid, and Anthropic is expected to have a massive "war chest" of $220 billion in cash post-IPO.

The deeper context lies in Anthropic's founding ethos. Its departure from OpenAI was rooted in a commitment to "safe AI," aiming to develop powerful models with robust guardrails. This IPO, however, introduces a new layer of complexity: the inherent tension between profit maximization for public shareholders and a public benefit mission, especially when the latter might require slowing down development or investing heavily in safety features that don't immediately generate revenue. The unusual corporate structure, with the Long-Term Benefit Trust, was designed to mitigate this, but experts question its true efficacy, drawing parallels to OpenAI's pre-Altman ouster governance issues. The concern is that the immense pressure to secure capital and win the "AI race" will inevitably lead to a de-prioritization of expensive AI safety initiatives and a push for faster commercialization. This directly feeds into the AI Governance and Regulation Gap storyline, as the industry's self-governance mechanisms are being tested under intense market pressure, underscoring the urgent need for robust external policy frameworks. Regardless of who "wins" the AI race, the compute suppliers (Amazon, Google, Nvidia, etc.) are positioned to profit, as all advanced AI models require their infrastructure, highlighting their foundational and increasingly powerful role in the global tech ecosystem.

SIIP: Instant Payment Use Grows in Africa Despite Concerns Over Fraud, AI

Source: Premium Times

What happened: A new report by the AfricaNenda Foundation, the State of Inclusive Instant Payment Systems (SIIPS), revealed that instant payment transaction volumes in Africa grew from 64.59 billion to 71.42 billion in 2025, with total transaction value rising to $1.828 trillion. This growth occurred despite increasing concerns over scams, fraud, and transaction errors; approximately 40% of sampled users experienced fraud. However, only 30% of these users reduced or stopped using digital payments, indicating that prompt resolution of disputes helps maintain trust. The report identified Artificial Intelligence (AI) as both an emerging threat and a potential solution. Fraudsters are increasingly using generative AI to create biometric verification artifacts and deepfakes to bypass KYC checks and trick users into authorizing push payments. Conversely, Nigerian and other African institutions are deploying AI-powered solutions to detect and prevent fraud. Examples include Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System (NIBSS) with its industry-wide AI-enabled fraud management platform, HAWK, and OPay's multi-layered scam alert system built around a machine-learning engine. Premier Oiwoh, NIBSS CEO, emphasized the critical need for intelligence sharing among stakeholders.

Deep Lore / Context: This report provides crucial insights into the evolving landscape of Fintech Diversification and Business Infrastructure in Africa, and critically, it highlights the dual role of AI in our Adversarial AI Evolution and Cybersecurity storyline. The continued growth of instant payment systems (IPS) in Africa, reaching significant transaction volumes and values, underscores the continent's rapid digital transformation and the increasing reliance on digital financial services. This growth is a testament to the convenience and accessibility offered by fintech innovations, which are driving financial inclusion across the continent. However, the high incidence of fraud (40% of users experiencing it) is a major concern, threatening to erode user trust and hinder further adoption. The finding that prompt dispute resolution mitigates this impact, with Nigeria's National Instant Payment (NIP) system cited as an example, emphasizes the importance of robust recourse mechanisms and customer service in the digital payment ecosystem.

The "Deep Lore" around AI's role is particularly compelling.
