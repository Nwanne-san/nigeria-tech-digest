### CBN Cuts MPR to 23%, Reshaping Economic Outlook and Carry-Trade Dynamics
**Source:** Nairametrics, BusinessDay

**What happened**
The Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) has cut its Monetary Policy Rate (MPR) by 350 basis points, bringing it down to 23%. This significant reduction, following a period of aggressive rate hikes, signals a pivot towards stimulating economic growth. The decision is expected to reshape investor allocation across fixed income and equities, with stockbrokers anticipating varied benefits across sectors. However, this hefty rate cut has eroded Nigeria's carry-trade edge against Ghana, making the West African peer a more attractive destination for portfolio capital inflows due to a reduced interest-rate differential. Interestingly, despite the rate cut, the Naira recorded slight gains in the parallel market on Wednesday.

**Deep Lore / Context**
This is a critical, multi-faceted development that directly continues our "CBN Policy and Economic Management" storyline, which has been escalating. The CBN's decision to cut the MPR to 23% from a higher rate (previously noted as 26.5% in prior briefings, making this a 350bps cut) marks a significant shift in monetary policy. For months, the CBN prioritized taming inflation and stabilizing the Naira through aggressive rate hikes, which, while attracting foreign portfolio investment and boosting foreign reserves (previously noted at $55 billion), also constrained credit growth and economic activity. This pivot suggests the CBN believes it has achieved some level of stability, particularly with the Naira's recent performance and foreign reserves surge, and is now shifting focus to growth stimulation.

The impact on carry-trade dynamics is a direct consequence of this policy shift. A higher interest rate differential typically attracts "hot money" from foreign investors seeking higher returns on short-term debt. By reducing this differential, Nigeria becomes less attractive compared to countries like Ghana, potentially leading to capital outflows or reduced inflows in the short term. However, the unexpected appreciation of the Naira in the black market despite the rate cut is a complex signal. It could indicate improved market confidence, reduced speculative activity, or a lagging effect of previous policies. This move by the CBN will have profound implications for borrowing costs for businesses, the profitability of banks, and the overall investment climate, all of which are crucial for the "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" storyline. It reflects a delicate balancing act between controlling inflation, attracting foreign capital, and fostering domestic economic expansion.

### African Startups Face Financing Gap Due to Lack of Business Legibility, Not Just Capital
**Source:** TechCabal

**What happened**
A recent analysis from the GEC+Africa conference in Cape Town suggests that Africa's startup funding problem is less about a shortage of capital and more about a "shortage of business legibility." While money exists in banks, VC funds, and other lenders, and millions of businesses generate revenue, much of this economic activity doesn't produce the reliable, structured data that financial institutions need to assess risk. This "information gap" means profitable businesses can still appear too risky to finance. Solutions are emerging, such as African Bank's efforts to turn fragmented small-business activity into usable information, and 22 On Sloane's KUMii, an AI-powered platform helping startups discover funding opportunities across a fragmented ecosystem.

**Deep Lore / Context**
This TechCabal report provides crucial insight into the "Nigerian tech ecosystem (startups, fintech)" storyline, which has been developing with discussions on funding and government initiatives. It reframes the long-standing challenge of access to finance for African startups and SMEs. Traditionally, the narrative has focused on a scarcity of capital, but this analysis points to a more fundamental issue: the inability of traditional financial systems to "see" and understand the risk profiles of many African businesses, especially smaller and informal ones. These businesses often lack standardized accounting, digital records, and integrated operational data, making them opaque to lenders and investors who rely on conventional metrics like collateral, business age, and predictable financial performance.

The emergence of solutions like African Bank's holistic support (payroll, tax, HR, invoice discounting) and 22 On Sloane's AI-powered KUMii platform is particularly significant. These initiatives are building the "information layer" between businesses and capital, demonstrating that technology, especially AI, can bridge this legibility gap. By integrating with operational systems, tracking real-time performance, and matching businesses with appropriate funding, these platforms are creating new financial infrastructure that moves beyond traditional models. This shift is vital for unlocking the true potential of Africa's entrepreneurial landscape, enabling more businesses to access the capital they need to grow, and fostering a more robust and inclusive digital economy. It highlights a key opportunity for tech developers to build solutions that address this fundamental data problem.
