### FG moves to turn Nigeria’s Startup Act into funding, tax and export gains for founders
**Source:** BusinessDay

**What happened**
The Federal Government is intensifying efforts to translate Nigeria's Startup Act from a legal framework into tangible benefits for startups, including funding, tax relief, export opportunities, and regulatory support. The National Information Technology Development Agency (NITDA), through its subsidiary the Office for Nigerian Digital Innovation (ONDI), convened a co-creation session in Abuja, emphasizing that the success of the law will now be measured by the ease with which startups and investors can access its incentives. NITDA Director-General Kashifu Inuwa, speaking through Victoria Fabunmi, National Coordinator of ONDI, stressed the need for government agencies, private-sector players, and other stakeholders to collaborate to remove institutional bottlenecks. The law, signed on October 19, 2022, contains over 31 incentives across six categories, involving more than 15 government institutions, making coordination a major challenge.

**Deep Lore / Context**
This development marks a crucial shift in the implementation of the Nigerian Startup Act, a policy that has been a beacon of hope for the tech ecosystem since its enactment. For years, the Nigerian tech space has clamored for a more supportive regulatory environment, one that acknowledges the unique needs of startups and fosters innovation. The Act was designed to address issues like access to capital, cumbersome regulations, and the need for a clear legal identity for startups. However, the initial euphoria was tempered by the slow pace of implementation, with many founders finding it difficult to navigate the bureaucratic maze to access the promised incentives. This renewed push, explicitly acknowledging the need to move "from designing the policy to delivering its benefits," reflects a recognition within government that a legal framework alone is insufficient; practical, accessible mechanisms are required. The involvement of over 15 MDAs highlights the cross-cutting nature of startup support, from finance and trade to communications and innovation, and the inherent complexity of achieving seamless coordination.

The significance of this move, which directly addresses the "Nigerian tech ecosystem (startups, fintech)" storyline, is immense. Nigerian startups attracted about $410 million in funding in 2024, a testament to the ecosystem's resilience despite broader economic pressures. The government views the Startup Act as a vital tool to deepen this investment pipeline and enhance the ability of local companies to scale and compete globally. The challenge now lies in operationalizing existing structures like the Startup Consultative Forum, the digital startup support engagement portal, and the Startup Investment Seed Fund framework. For a web developer growing into AI engineering, this could mean more accessible grants, tax breaks for innovative tech companies, and a more streamlined path to market for AI-driven solutions. The call for continuous engagement with startups and investors is also a positive signal, suggesting a more adaptive policy environment that responds to the evolving needs of the ecosystem. The ultimate test will be whether entrepreneurs can genuinely obtain tax relief, funding, and regulatory support without encountering the very bureaucratic barriers the Act was intended to dismantle.

### Catholic priests reject FG’s ranching plan for Benue
**Source:** Vanguard

**What happened**
The Nigeria Catholic Diocesan Priests Association (NCDPA), Makurdi Diocese, has vehemently rejected the Federal Government's decision to include Benue State in the pilot implementation of its National Ranching Policy. In a statement signed by its Chairman, Rev. Fr. Joseph Beba, the NCDPA described Benue as "an already bleeding and targeted state," expressing deep concern that the plan could threaten ancestral lands and the security of residents. The Minister of Livestock Development, Alhaji Idi Maiha, had previously identified five states, including Benue and the FCT, for the policy's pilot phase. The priests criticized the alleged exclusion of the state government from the decision-making process, stating, "Had the elected leadership of Benue State been genuinely consulted, we believe it would never have consented to any arrangement capable of undermining the rights, dignity, security and ancestral heritage of our people." They warned that ranching must not become "a disguised instrument for land grabbing, forced occupation or the permanent displacement of indigenous communities," and declared that "our land is not for sale and has never been on sale."

**Deep Lore / Context**
This rejection by the Catholic priests in Benue is a significant escalation in the ongoing "Nigeria's Infrastructure for Insecurity Response" storyline, particularly concerning the persistent farmer-herder conflicts and land disputes that have plagued the state. Benue, often referred to as the "Food Basket of the Nation," has endured years of deadly clashes, leading to mass killings, displacement of communities, and destruction of livelihoods. The state has long advocated for anti-open grazing laws, which have been met with resistance in some quarters but are seen by many residents as crucial for peace and security. The Federal Government's National Ranching Policy is intended to modernize livestock management and reduce conflicts, but its implementation in Benue without what the priests consider genuine consultation with state and community leaders is viewed with deep suspicion. The NCDPA's strong language, warning against "land grabbing" and "forced occupation," reflects the profound distrust and historical grievances surrounding land ownership and resource control in the region.

The core of the conflict in Benue and other Middle Belt states is often framed as a struggle over land and resources, exacerbated by climate change, population growth, and a breakdown of traditional conflict resolution mechanisms. The priests' assertion that "Ranching in Benue State is therefore not viable, and we reject this project idea with all legal and spiritual resources" underscores the deep-seated opposition rooted in the agrarian nature of the state and the perceived threat to indigenous communities' ancestral heritage. This development highlights the complex interplay between federal policy, state autonomy, community rights, and the ever-present challenge of insecurity. The call for the safe return of Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) and the restoration of their access to ancestral lands further emphasizes the humanitarian crisis linked to these conflicts. For the government, imposing a policy without broad local buy-in risks exacerbating tensions rather than resolving them, potentially undermining efforts to restore peace and stability in a region critical to Nigeria's food security.
