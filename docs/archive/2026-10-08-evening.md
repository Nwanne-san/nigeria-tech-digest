#### 1. FG Rejects Fuel Subsidy Return, Citing N20 Trillion Annual Cost and Economic Risks
**Headline:** Fuel subsidy would cost Nigeria over N20trn yearly — Minister (Premium Times)
**What happened:** The Federal Government has firmly rejected calls for the return of petrol subsidy, with Minister of Finance and Coordinating Minister of the Economy, Taiwo Oyedele, warning that restoring petrol to its pre-2023 reform price would cost Nigeria over N20 trillion annually. Speaking at a press briefing in Abuja, Oyedele explained that Nigeria consumes about 50 million litres of petrol daily, and even a proposal to sell petrol at N500 per litre would cost over N16 trillion annually, before accounting for increased consumption and smuggling. He stressed that such amounts would consume nearly all the Federation Account shared among all three tiers of government in 2025, diverting funds from salaries, pensions, schools, hospitals, and security. Oyedele further warned that a return to subsidy could weaken government revenues, trigger a sovereign credit downgrade, increase borrowing costs, and push the exchange rate towards N3,000 to a dollar, ultimately leading to petrol prices of at least N2,000 per litre. He defended the 2023 subsidy removal, stating it saved N15.8 trillion for the Federation Account between June 2023 and December 2025, with N10.4 trillion going to states and local governments.
**Deep Lore / Context:** This strong rejection of a full subsidy return is a critical **escalation** in the "CBN Policy and Economic Management" and "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" storylines. It directly addresses the persistent public and political pressure to alleviate the economic hardship caused by the initial subsidy removal, which a presidential aide had previously admitted deepened poverty. Minister Oyedele's detailed breakdown of the potential costs – N20 trillion annually for a full return, or N16 trillion for a N500/litre price point – provides a stark economic argument against reversing the policy. These figures are not just abstract numbers; they represent the entire revenue base for the country, highlighting the immense fiscal burden that a subsidy regime would impose.

The minister's warning about the cascading effects of a subsidy return – sovereign credit downgrade, increased borrowing costs, pressure on foreign reserves, and a potential N3,000/\$ exchange rate – underscores the government's commitment to fiscal discipline and market-driven reforms, despite their painful immediate consequences. This stance is consistent with the broader efforts to stabilize the Naira, which has seen some bullish trends against the British Pound, as noted in our "CBN Policy and Economic Management" storyline. The government's argument is that while the short-term pain is significant, reversing course would lead to even greater long-term economic instability, including a higher pump price for petrol eventually, as the government would be subsidizing foreign exchange. This sets the stage for a continued ideological battle between advocates for market reforms and those prioritizing immediate social welfare, a debate that will undoubtedly intensify as the 2027 elections approach.
