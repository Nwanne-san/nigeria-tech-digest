3. Presidency Dismisses Claims Tinubu Borrowed ₦120 Trillion in Three Years, Cites Exchange Rate and Debt Restructuring

Source: Punch / The Sun

What happened The Presidency, through Special Assistant to the President on Social Media, Dada Olusegun, dismissed claims that President Bola Tinubu borrowed ₦120 trillion in three years while also removing the fuel subsidy. Olusegun described these figures as "Opposition’s propaganda machines" and a distortion of Nigeria’s public debt records. He clarified that the ₦46 trillion figure attributed to 16 past leaders at the end of 2022 excluded ₦22.7 trillion in Central Bank "Ways and Means" overdrafts, which were only officially recognised in May 2023. Furthermore, he explained that the unification of the exchange rate in June 2023 caused the naira value of existing dollar debt to instantly appear ₦13.4 trillion larger on paper without new borrowing. In actual dollar terms, Nigeria’s public debt rose from $113.4 billion in June 2023 to $120.9 billion by June 2026, an increase of only $7.5 billion.

Deep Lore / Context This rebuttal from the Presidency is a direct response to the ongoing and escalating debate around Nigeria's "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" and "CBN Policy and Economic Management" storylines, particularly concerning the nation's rising debt burden. The claims being dismissed originated from viral social media graphics that compared cumulative borrowing under previous administrations with the Tinubu government's record, often without adequate context. The Presidency's explanation highlights two critical factors that often distort public perception of debt figures: the securitisation of previously off-book Central Bank advances (Ways and Means) and the significant impact of exchange rate unification. The "Ways and Means" advances represent a form of fiscal dominance, where the CBN directly finances government deficits, a practice that has long been criticised for its inflationary effects and its contribution to naira depreciation, as highlighted in a recent CBN study we tracked. Bringing these advances onto the official debt books, while increasing the headline figure, is a move towards greater fiscal transparency, albeit one that makes the current administration appear to have borrowed more.

The impact of the naira float and exchange rate unification is equally significant. When the official exchange rate was artificially pegged, the naira value of foreign debt was understated. The move to a market-reflective rate, while a necessary reform for attracting foreign investment and improving FX liquidity, instantly inflated the naira equivalent of existing dollar-denominated debt. This is a crucial distinction that often gets lost in public discourse and political rhetoric. The government's defence also points to the positive impact of fuel subsidy removal on Federation Account Allocation Committee (FAAC) funds for states and local governments, rising from ₦656 billion in April 2023 to ₦2.4 trillion by September 2026. This is a core argument for the economic reforms, aiming to free up funds for critical development. However, the debate continues, with opposition parties like the Nigeria Democratic Congress criticising fresh borrowing plans, raising concerns about the financial burden on future generations. This ongoing narrative battle over economic data and policy effectiveness will undoubtedly shape the political discourse leading up to 2027.
