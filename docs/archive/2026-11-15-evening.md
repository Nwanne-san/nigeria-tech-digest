### 1. State Police: NASS Progresses Despite Four States' Delay
**Source:** Premium Times

**What happened:** Senate Leader Opeyemi Bamidele announced that four of Nigeria's 36 state Houses of Assembly have yet to transmit their resolutions on the proposed creation of state police to the National Assembly. Despite this, Mr. Bamidele, representing Ekiti Central Senatorial District, affirmed that their failure to comply would not impede federal lawmakers from proceeding with the process and forwarding the bill to the president for assent. He stated this on Friday at an award ceremony in Abuja, noting, "We are now expecting four State Houses of Assembly to turn in their reports on or before the National Assembly reconvenes on November 17, 2026. This suggests that next week will be decisive and defining for the National Assembly." This indicates that the proposal has met the constitutional requirement of approval by at least two-thirds (24) of the state assemblies.

**Deep Lore / Context:** This development marks a significant step in the long-standing debate over state police, a storyline we've been tracking under "Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)" and "Security Operations and Insecurity Challenges (Nigeria)" as "escalating." The idea of state police has gained considerable traction due to the persistent and escalating insecurity across the country, from banditry and kidnapping to farmer-herder clashes. Proponents argue that localized policing would be more effective in addressing community-specific security threats, fostering better intelligence gathering, and improving response times. The current federal police structure is often criticized for being overstretched, underfunded, and lacking sufficient local knowledge and accountability. The push for state police has been a key political factor, with various regional groups and state governors advocating for it as a solution to the perceived failure of centralized security.

However, the journey to state police is fraught with complex challenges and concerns. Critics, including some federal lawmakers and civil society organizations, have raised fears about potential abuse by state governors, the risk of creating "state armies" that could be used for political intimidation, and the exacerbation of ethnic or religious tensions. There are also significant questions regarding funding, training, and operational standards to ensure professionalism and prevent human rights abuses. The Senate Leader's assurance that the National Assembly will "move forward and fulfil the aspiration of our people" underscores the political will behind this reform, but also highlights the potential for some states to resist or delay, possibly due to concerns about resource implications or the political ramifications of ceding control. The proposed framework aims to establish state-led, community-anchored policing within clear constitutional safeguards, prohibiting the conversion of existing local security outfits like Amotekun or Hisbah into state police or allowing them to bear firearms. This balancing act between local control and federal oversight will be crucial in shaping the final legislation and its implementation.
