Their inclusion follows a Supreme Court judgment on September 24, 2026, which affirmed key provisions of the Electoral Act 2026, specifically Sections 77(5)–(7) and 84(2), requiring parties to submit membership registers 21 days before primaries and ensuring only registered members can vote or be voted for.

**Deep Lore / Context:** This development is a direct continuation of the **"Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)"** storyline, marking a significant escalation in INEC's enforcement of electoral laws. This ruling re-established the strict requirement for political parties to adhere to their submitted membership registers and nomination procedures. The controversy had been brewing since the Zenith Labour Party challenged these provisions, arguing they infringed on party autonomy. However, the apex court sided with INEC, giving the commission a powerful legal backing to scrutinize candidate eligibility. This strict adherence to the Electoral Act has been a long-standing demand from civil society and election observers, aiming to curb internal party wrangling, carpet-crossing, and the often-opaque processes of candidate selection that have historically plagued Nigerian elections. The inclusion of these high-profile names, despite their complex political movements and primary disputes, suggests that their parties managed to align their processes with the newly reinforced legal framework, or that their specific circumstances fell outside the disqualifying criteria.

The implications of this stricter enforcement are profound for Nigeria's political landscape. For politicians like Mohammed Adamu, who defected from the APC to SDP and emerged as a candidate through a substitution primary, his inclusion on the list validates his party's process under the new rules. While their inclusion offers a measure of stability for these candidates, the underlying legal challenges and interpretations will undoubtedly continue to shape pre-election litigation, as parties and aspirants test the boundaries of the Supreme Court's pronouncement. ### World Bank: Debt Interest Payments Outstrip Health and Education Budgets in 80% of African Countries, Including Nigeria
**Headline:** World Bank: Debt interest swallows health, education budgets in 80% of African countries (Nairametrics)
**What happened:** The World Bank's October 2026 Africa Economic Update revealed that interest payments on public debt now exceed government spending on health and/or education in nearly four-fifths of sub-Saharan African countries. This alarming trend is due to high debt-servicing costs, which continue to constrain public finances across the region. Interest payments averaged between 2.9% and 3.2% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) from 2023 to 2026. Despite a projected decline in the region's overall fiscal deficit from 5.6% of GDP in 2020 to 3.5% in 2026, and a further narrowing to 3.1% by 2028, debt-servicing costs severely limit resources for other priorities. Nigeria's own debt-service burden provides a national perspective, with N16 trillion spent in 2025 (up 22.9% from 2024) and an additional N10.61 trillion incurred between June 2023 and December 2025, exceeding strategic infrastructure spending.

**Deep Lore / Context:** This World Bank report is a critical update to the **"CBN Policy and Economic Management"** and **"National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)"** storylines, highlighting the severe fiscal constraints facing Nigeria and many other African nations. While Fitch recently revised Nigeria's outlook to positive based on CBN reforms and rising FX reserves, this report presents a more sobering reality from a public welfare perspective. The core issue is that the cost of servicing existing debt, rather than new borrowing, is increasingly consuming a disproportionate share of national budgets. This "crowding out" effect means that funds desperately needed for human capital development—health and education—are being diverted to debt obligations. For a country like Nigeria with a large and growing youth population, underinvestment in these sectors has long-term consequences for productivity, social stability, and poverty reduction.

The report's details on Nigeria's debt-service burden are particularly concerning. Spending N16 trillion on debt servicing in 2025, and incurring N10.61 trillion in additional costs due to exchange-rate depreciation and higher domestic interest rates, underscores the vulnerability of the economy to external shocks and internal policy choices. The fact that these additional debt costs exceeded spending on strategic infrastructure development over the same period (June 2023-December 2025) is a stark indicator of misaligned priorities forced by fiscal realities. The shift towards domestic borrowing as the dominant source of government financing since 2021, while reducing external exposure, comes with its own challenges: higher interest rates and shorter maturities, increasing refinancing pressures. This situation creates a vicious cycle where economic growth is hampered by a lack of investment in critical sectors, while debt continues to mount, making it harder to break free from the cycle. Addressing this will require not just fiscal discipline but also innovative strategies for revenue generation and debt restructuring.
