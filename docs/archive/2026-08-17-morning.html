Headline: Pointless Debate on Tinubu’s Certificate Distracts from Substantive Issues, Says Columnist (Vanguard)

What happened: A recent opinion piece by Suleiman A. Suleiman in Vanguard argues that the ongoing debate surrounding President Bola Ahmed Tinubu’s educational certificates is a "pointless distraction" that is preventing Nigerians from engaging with more critical issues of governance and policy ahead of the 2027 elections. The author contends that for an incumbent president, the certificate controversy is not a useful measure of eligibility or performance. He highlights that while the 2015 controversy around former President Buhari's certificate focused on a single issue (lack of secondary school certificate), Tinubu's case involves three: the alleged lack of primary/secondary certificates, how he gained university admission without them, and the more serious allegation of forgery regarding his Chicago State University degree. Suleiman asserts that higher educational qualifications, like a university degree or military training, should supersede lower ones, making the debate on minimum educational thresholds redundant once INEC accepts a candidate's submission.

Deep Lore / Context: This story continues the "Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)" storyline, which has been escalating due to persistent concerns over electoral offences, vote-buying, and allegations of forged documents. The debate over President Tinubu's academic credentials has been a recurring feature of Nigerian politics, particularly since the 2023 presidential election. Critics and opposition figures have repeatedly questioned the authenticity and completeness of his academic records, with the Chicago State University degree being a particular point of contention. The author's argument reflects a segment of public discourse that views these certificate issues as a diversion from the more pressing economic and security challenges facing the nation.

The core of the "Deep Lore" here lies in Nigeria's constitutional requirements for presidential candidates, specifically Section 131(d) which mandates education "up to at least School Certificate level or its equivalent," and Section 318(1)(d) which grants INEC latitude to determine acceptable equivalent qualifications. Past controversies, such as that involving former President Muhammadu Buhari, established a precedent where extensive military training and public service were deemed sufficient equivalents. However, the current debate around Tinubu is amplified by the specific allegation of forgery, which, if conclusively proven, would carry far greater legal and political weight. The author emphasizes that only a court or Chicago State University itself can definitively rule on the forgery claim, and without such a determination, the debate remains speculative. This ongoing saga underscores a broader challenge in Nigerian politics: the tendency for electoral disputes to devolve into personal attacks and credential challenges, rather than focusing on policy alternatives and governance records, especially as the 2027 election cycle begins to take shape.

Deep Lore / Context: This development highlights a recurring tension in Nigeria regarding the management and funding of public institutions, particularly in the education sector. Parents' concerns about the quality of teachers under a new management structure, despite KCOBA's assurances of "strengthening governance and improving learning outcomes," reflect a deep-seated distrust.
