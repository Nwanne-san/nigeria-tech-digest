**Deep Lore / Context:**
This announcement is a direct response to long-standing concerns about the integrity of Nigeria's electoral processes, a core component of the **Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)** storyline. INEC's explicit commitment to reject discrepant results from IREV signals a renewed effort to address these issues and rebuild public trust in the electoral system. This move is critical because the perceived fairness of elections is fundamental to political stability and democratic legitimacy in Nigeria.

The emphasis on BVAS data as the benchmark for IREV uploads underscores the importance of technology in safeguarding electoral integrity. The BVAS, designed for voter accreditation and electronic transmission of results, is intended to minimize human interference and enhance accuracy. By making BVAS data the definitive standard, INEC is attempting to close loopholes that have historically been exploited for electoral manipulation. However, the effectiveness of this policy will depend on rigorous implementation, the capacity of INEC officials, and the robustness of the underlying technology. This development is particularly relevant as early political maneuvering for the 2027 elections intensifies, with various parties already signaling campaign readiness. ### CBN Bills Surge to N5.6 Trillion, Driving 74% of September's Money Supply Increase
**Headline:** CBN bills surge to N5.6 trillion, drive 74% of money supply increase in September (Nairametrics)
**What happened:** Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) bills held by money-holding sectors dramatically increased to N5.60 trillion in September 2026, a massive jump from N9.68 billion in August. This surge accounted for 74.4% of the N7.51 trillion monthly increase in Nigeria's broad money supply (M3), which reached N146.60 trillion. While M3 expanded by 5.40% month-on-month, the narrower M2 measure, excluding CBN bills, only rose by 1.38% to N141.00 trillion, indicating that the exceptional rise in CBN bills was the primary driver of the headline money supply growth. The CBN's liquidity management operations, particularly the sale of N17.51 trillion in OMO bills against N10.89 trillion in maturing bills, led to a net liquidity withdrawal of approximately N6.62 trillion.
**Deep Lore / Context:**
This report provides crucial insight into the **CBN Policy and Economic Management** storyline, which has been escalating with the CBN's recent decision to reduce the Monetary Policy Rate (MPR) to 23% to stimulate economic activity, while simultaneously managing liquidity through OMO bills. The significant increase in CBN bills reflects the apex bank's aggressive use of Open Market Operations (OMO) to absorb excess liquidity from the financial system. This strategy is typically employed to manage inflation, stabilize the Naira, and control the money supply. However, the data reveals a nuanced picture: while headline money supply (M3) appears to grow substantially, a large portion of this growth is tied up in these interest-bearing CBN bills, rather than representing an equivalent increase in funds readily available for transactions, savings, and credit creation in the real economy. This distinction is critical for policymakers balancing inflation control with the need to support productive economic activity.

The composition of money supply, with CBN bills accounting for the bulk of M3 expansion, suggests that the CBN is actively trying to sterilize liquidity. This could be a response to inflationary pressures or efforts to manage foreign exchange demand. The fact that M2, which more closely reflects immediately spendable monetary balances, grew at a much slower rate (1.38%) indicates that the direct impact on consumer spending and private sector credit might be more moderate than the M3 figure suggests. This complex interplay of monetary tools highlights the challenges the CBN faces in navigating Nigeria's economic landscape, where the goals of price stability, exchange rate management, and economic growth often present trade-offs. The figures also point to rising government credit and a marginal monthly decline in private-sector credit, further complicating the economic outlook.
