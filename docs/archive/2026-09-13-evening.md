### Presidency Rejects Atiku's Call to Reinstate Fuel Subsidy, Citing Economic Reforms and Investment Risks
**Source:** Daily Trust

**What happened:** The Presidency has firmly rejected former Vice-President Atiku Abubakar's proposal to reinstate fuel subsidy if elected in 2027, stating that such a move would "undermine the reforms already undertaken in the petroleum sector." He highlighted that the petroleum landscape has fundamentally changed since President Bola Tinubu announced the subsidy removal on May 29, 2023. The Minister of Finance, Taiwo Oyedele, added that the subsidy removal has generated N15.8 trillion for the federation between June 2023 and December 2025, with N5.4 trillion accruing to the Federal Government and N10.4 trillion shared among states and local governments.

**Deep Lore / Context:** This is a direct continuation of the "CBN Policy and Economic Management" (status: escalating) and "National Progress & Economy (Nigeria)" (status: escalating) storylines, specifically the ongoing debate about the economic impact of the fuel subsidy removal and the government's fiscal strategy. The removal of fuel subsidy was one of the first major policy decisions of the Tinubu administration, leading to a significant increase in petrol prices (from below N200 to above N1,000) and a corresponding surge in transportation, food, and living costs, which has severely impacted household well-being. This policy was initially supported by Atiku Abubakar during the 2023 campaigns, making his current stance a notable shift in the lead-up to the 2027 elections, further intensifying the "Political Integrity and Electoral Processes (Nigeria)" storyline (status: escalating).

The Presidency's argument against reinstatement centers on the potential for legal and fiscal complications, and the discouragement of investment in domestic refining, including the Dangote Refinery and other modular refineries. This connects to the broader national ambition of achieving energy independence and reducing reliance on imported refined products, a key component of economic progress. Financial experts like Prof. Uche Uwaleke and Prof. Ken Ife have also weighed in, emphasizing the unsustainability of the old subsidy regime, which was a massive burden on public finances and a hotbed for arbitrage and rent-seeking. They argue that the success of subsidy removal should be measured by its ability to foster a more productive economy and better quality of life, rather than just stopping payments. The public's experience, however, as voiced by civil servants like Ibrahim Abbas, points to "economic hardship and a huge depletion of the purchasing power of the Naira," making Atiku's proposal attractive to ordinary Nigerians despite its potential long-term drawbacks. The debate underscores the difficult balance between immediate public relief and long-term economic sustainability, a core challenge in Nigeria's economic management.
