### SEC DG Urges Universities to Explore Capital Market Funding
**Source:** Vanguard, Nairametrics

**What happened**
Dr. Emomotimi Agama, the Director-General of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), called on Nigerian universities to shift away from their reliance on government allocations and instead embrace capital formation, investment, and ownership through the capital market. Speaking at the University of Ibadan Alumni Association Annual Public Service Lecture, themed "First and Best But Whose Capital Built It? Rethinking How Nigeria Funds Its Own Future," Agama highlighted that the University of Ibadan, despite its legacy and producing numerous national leaders, has never approached the capital market to raise long-term funds. He contrasted the N4.65 trillion raised by banks in 24 months through recapitalisation with the N2.53 billion allocated to a university by TETFund, demonstrating the vast difference in funding approaches. Agama proposed five financing instruments, including a properly constituted endowment fund, the issuance of bonds and sukuk backed by revenue-generating assets like student housing (potentially through a Real Estate Investment Trust or concession), a university innovation fund for equity stakes in spin-off companies, and a diaspora-targeted investment instrument.

**Deep Lore / Context**
This call from the SEC DG is a significant development in the long-running narrative of underfunded public education in Nigeria, a storyline that has seen frequent labor disputes, strikes by resident doctors and university staff (as we noted in our October 6th brief, "Public Health Crises in Nigeria" remains 'developing' due to these ongoing strikes), and a general decline in educational infrastructure. The traditional dependence on government appropriations has proven unsustainable, especially given Nigeria's current fiscal realities. The SEC's intervention signals a policy shift towards encouraging self-sufficiency and leveraging domestic capital for development. Nigeria possesses substantial pension assets, reported at N31.48 trillion as of July 2026, and a total market capitalisation on the Nigerian Exchange (NGX) of N215.09 trillion. The challenge, as Agama pointed out, isn't a scarcity of capital but a failure to translate it into productive investments.

The SEC DG's proposals are designed to tap into these available long-term funds, offering universities a pathway to financial independence and infrastructural development. By suggesting instruments like endowment funds, bonds, REITs for student housing, and innovation funds, Agama is pushing for a paradigm shift from a "consumption" model of donations to a "capital growth" model. This move is critical for the future of Nigerian education, as it could unlock significant resources for research, infrastructure, and improved learning environments, directly impacting the quality of graduates, including those in tech and AI fields. It also implicitly addresses the brain drain, as better-funded institutions could retain talent and provide world-class training. The emphasis on financial discipline, audited accounts, and credit ratings underscores a push for transparency and accountability, which are essential for attracting capital market investors. This initiative aligns with broader national goals of economic diversification and human capital development, aiming to create a more robust and self-sustaining educational sector.

### AI Boom Lifts Trade but Raises Financial Risks – UNCTAD
**Source:** Premium Times

**What happened**
The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) has warned that while the artificial intelligence (AI) boom is a significant driver of global merchandise trade, its growing concentration in technology-related goods and investment exposes the global economy to substantial financial risks if demand for AI hardware slows. According to UNCTAD's latest Trade and Development Report 2026, global trade in goods and services grew by 4.4% in real terms in 2025 and is projected to expand by about 4% in 2026. However, AI-related goods, such as chips and servers for data centers, accounted for about one-sixth of global goods trade by value in 2025 and contributed 42% of its growth. UNCTAD cautioned that the AI boom shows "signs of earlier financial bubbles," and a fall in share prices could trigger widespread selling and squeeze credit beyond the technology sector. Investment in AI remains highly concentrated in China and the United States, with significant spending on advanced chips and model development, generating relatively little employment despite stimulating economic activity through data centers.

**Deep Lore / Context**
This UNCTAD report provides a crucial global economic context for the "AI Existential Risk and Regulation Debate" and the "AI Governance and Regulation Gap" storylines, by highlighting the macroeconomic implications and potential systemic risks associated with the rapid, yet concentrated, growth of the AI sector. While the AI boom is undeniably driving global trade and technological advancement, UNCTAD's warning about "signs of earlier financial bubbles" echoes concerns previously raised about the rapid valuation of AI companies (e.g., TypeSafe AI's Jev valued at $7.5B). The concentration of investment in a few key areas (chips, model development) and geographies (US, China) creates a narrow base for this growth, making the global economy vulnerable to a sudden downturn in AI demand or investment.

For Nigeria, this report underscores both opportunities and risks. While Nigeria is actively pursuing "Nigeria's Digital Skills and AI Workforce Development," the country's direct participation in the high-value AI hardware trade and concentrated investment flows is minimal. This means Nigeria is largely a consumer or adopter of AI technologies rather than a primary producer of core AI infrastructure. The risk of a global AI bubble bursting could have indirect but significant impacts on Nigeria's economy through reduced global trade, tighter credit conditions, and a potential slowdown in foreign investment, even if not directly in the tech sector. The report implicitly calls for a more diversified and inclusive approach to AI development and investment globally, and for countries like Nigeria, it emphasizes the need to focus on leveraging AI for local problem-solving and building resilient, diversified economies that are less susceptible to single-sector shocks. It also reinforces the global calls for stricter regulation and transparency in the AI sector to mitigate these financial and systemic risks.
